A sensor doesn't speak PLC.

06 — 09 · The Industrial Standard

Current vs. voltage, and why 4–20 mA wins

In a current loop, the same current flows through every point of the series circuit no matter how long the cable is — voltage drop along the wire doesn't corrupt the reading the way it can with a voltage signal. That single fact explains why current transmission became the industrial default over long runs.

Property4–20 mA0–10 V
Signal typeCurrentVoltage
Long-distance industrial useExcellentMore sensitive to drop/noise
Two-wire transmittersCommonLess suited
Live zeroYesUsually no

Neither is "better" in the abstract — 0–10 V suits short runs inside a panel; 4–20 mA earns its place on long industrial cable runs.

Live zero

0% of range   →   4 mA  (live zero)
Possible fault →   0 mA  (outside valid range)

A true 0% reading still carries 4 mA — a live signal the system can validate. A reading of 0 mA sits outside the valid range entirely and can be flagged as a probable fault. It isn't a universal guarantee, though — what 0 mA actually means still depends on the instrument and how the input is configured.

13 — 15 · Cleaning the Signal Up

Signal conditioning

Raw sensor outputs are often not directly suitable for a controller. A sensor producing 20 mV feeding a PLC that requires 0–10 V needs real circuitry in between:

Sensor (20 mV) → Amplifier → Filter → Isolation → 0–10 V → PLC

Amplification

Gain G = V_out / V_in. 10 mV in, 1 V out → G = 100. The opposite operation, attenuation, reduces an oversized signal before it reaches sensitive electronics.

Filtering — separating signal from noise

Every real signal is really two signals superimposed: useful signal + noise. Watch the raw noisy line settle into a clean trace as a low-pass filter removes the fast-changing noise while preserving the slow-changing real signal — exactly what happens with a noisy temperature measurement.

16 — 18 · Protecting the Signal

Isolation, ground loops, and differential measurement

A ground loop happens when two devices share both a signal path and a separate ground path that aren't at exactly the same potential. That small difference drives an unwanted current through the signal wire itself, corrupting the reading. An isolation transformer breaks the unwanted conductive path entirely.

top: ground loop present → noisy signal   |   bottom: proper isolation → clean signal

A differential input responds to the difference between two conductors, so common-mode noise picked up equally by both wires cancels out — exactly what twisting the pair achieves at the physical level.

19 — 20 · Getting a Usable Number

Linearization & the Wheatstone bridge

Some sensors — strain gauges, RTDs, load cells — are entirely passive and need external excitation just to produce a signal at all. The classic circuit for turning a tiny resistance change into a clean, measurable voltage is the Wheatstone bridge.

V_out ≈ 0 mV

When all four arms are equal, the bridge is balanced and V_out = 0. Replace one arm with a strain gauge: even a microscopic resistance change unbalances the bridge and produces a small but measurable differential voltage — proportional to the strain. This is the circuit hiding inside most load cells.

21 — 22 · Becoming a Number

Analog-to-digital conversion

Analog signal → Sample → Quantize → Encode → Digital value

A 12-bit ADC has 2¹² = 4096 possible codes. For a 0–10 V input, the ideal code width is 10 / 4096 ≈ 2.44 mV — the theoretical resolution. Real systems add noise and nonlinearity on top of that ideal number.

SAMPLING
ALIASING

Left: a continuous signal represented by discrete samples, then quantized to fixed steps. Right: when sampling is too infrequent, a genuinely fast signal can produce sample points indistinguishable from a much slower one — the reason anti-aliasing filters exist ahead of every real ADC.

23 — 25 · Trusting the Number

Scaling, integrity, and fault signatures

Signal ≠ raw PLC count ≠ engineering unit. A perfectly healthy signal, scaled with the wrong range values, still produces a wrong answer.
Signal conditionLikely meaning
< 4 mAUnderrange or fault
> 20 mAOverrange
0 mAWire break / loss of power (instrument-dependent)
Unstable / stuckWiring, EMI, or sensor failure

Exact thresholds always depend on the specific instrument and how its input is configured — these are signatures to investigate, not guarantees.
